Weekday vs. Saturday Weddings: What Couples Actually Save

The same ballroom quoted two prices. Saturday: $8,000. Tuesday: $5,500. The chandeliers did not change, the kitchen did not change, and the dance floor did not change. The only thing that changed was the day — and the guest list that would show up on it.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

The same ballroom quoted two prices. Saturday: $8,000. Tuesday: $5,500. The chandeliers did not change, the kitchen did not change, and the dance floor did not change. The only thing that changed was the day — and the guest list that would show up on it. That pair of numbers, from a venue pricing guide, is the whole weekday-wedding argument in miniature: a real discount, on a real room, with a real cost attached that the quote does not show. The couples who take the Tuesday price are not buying a cheaper wedding. They are buying the same wedding on a day fewer people can attend, and the question is whether the $2,500 is worth the empty chairs.

Start with the day-by-day arithmetic, because the discounts are not uniform and the pattern matters more than any single figure. Saturday is the baseline, the most expensive day to marry, and about 75 percent of weddings are still held on Saturdays. Friday and Sunday typically run 15 to 25 percent below Saturday rates. Monday through Thursday cost the least, at 20 to 40 percent below Saturday, with some venues discounting weekday rates by 20 to 60 percent. The Knot’s 2026 Real Weddings Study puts numbers on the averages: Thursday weddings average around $31,100, against roughly $33,100 to $33,200 on weekends. Weekday weddings now represent 22 percent of all weddings, and the share is growing — not because couples have discovered a secret, but because the price gap has grown too wide to ignore.

The venue is where the saving is concentrated, and the published rates make it concrete. Venue Answers, tracking venues around Louisville, found two that publish weekday pricing: The Refinery in Jeffersonville, Indiana, charges $4,750 for a peak Saturday and $2,750 on a weekday, a saving of $2,000; Highland Oaks in Palmyra, Indiana, charges $5,995 on Saturday and $2,900 on a weekday, a saving of $3,095. In Kansas City, Complete Wedo reports Friday weddings saving 15 to 30 percent off the Saturday rate at many venues, and tells the story of a couple who took a Friday the 9th for $2,000 less than the Saturday they wanted. A Sunday wedding can cut venue fees by up to $4,000. These are not national averages. They are printed prices from named venues, and they are the numbers a couple can take into a negotiation.

The vendors follow the venue, but not by the same amount, and the differences decide whether the saving survives. Photographers and videographers typically discount weekdays by 10 to 15 percent, because their post-production time is the same regardless of the day and weekdays are otherwise spent editing, meeting clients, and second-shooting. DJs and bands, who have nothing to sell on a Tuesday night, may offer 20 to 30 percent off weekday rates. Catering is the category that disappoints: food and bar cost about the same on any day of the week, and the saving comes from lower minimum spends rather than lower prices — a venue that asks for a $12,000 minimum food and drink spend on a Saturday might set the weekday minimum closer to $7,000 or $8,500. The pattern is consistent across the guides: negotiate hardest on the venue and the entertainment, expect modest savings on photography and catering, and ask every vendor for its weekday rate in writing before comparing anything.

Add it up and the total is smaller than the venue discount suggests, which is the fact the weekday pitch usually leaves out. The venue rental is the part that drops; food and bar, which together with the venue make up more than half the budget, cost about the same on any day. Venue Answers puts the total saving at something like 10 to 15 percent, not half, and other planning sources put the overall figure at 10 to 30 percent. Plana.wedding, working in pounds, estimates a couple spending £25,000 on a Saturday wedding could spend £16,000 to £19,000 for the equivalent weekday celebration — a saving of £6,000 to £9,000, or roughly 25 to 35 percent. Under Der Linden, a Singapore venue, puts the saving at $3,000 to $10,000 for a fifty-guest celebration. The British and Singaporean figures are labeled as such; the American range is 10 to 15 percent on the total bill, and the venue saving of $2,000 to $3,000 is the part of it a couple can count on.

What the saving costs is attendance, and the cost is not theoretical. Guests take time off work, travel is more awkward, and some people simply decline — which is itself a hidden saving, since every guest who declines is about $292 the couple does not spend. But a celebration with thirty fewer guests is not the same celebration at a lower price; it is a different event, and the couple may feel the difference more than the savings. Friday is the compromise most couples actually take: guests need a half-day off, not a full one, and the venue is 15 to 25 percent cheaper. Sunday is the other compromise, and its cost arrives the next morning, when guests face work. Monday through Thursday is the deepest discount and the hardest ask, and it works best when most of the guest list is local and the day runs shorter anyway.

There is an upside the discount does not advertise. The first-choice vendors are actually free on weekdays. Top photographers, videographers, and bands often book their Saturdays a year or more in advance, and a couple marrying on a Thursday can hire the photographer who was unavailable on every Saturday they asked about. Venues that are booked solid on Saturdays two years out have Fridays available next spring, in Kansas City and in most other markets. The weekday is not only the cheaper day. For a couple with a short timeline — under six months, say — it is often the only day the venue they want is available at all.

Here is the counterargument, and it is the one the discount leaves out. A weekday wedding is cheaper partly because it is harder to attend, and venues discount weekdays because demand is thin, not from generosity. The vendors who offer the steepest weekday cuts are often the ones with the thinnest Saturday books, and a discount is information: it says something about what is being sold, and the buyer should ask what it is. There is also a cheaper lever that has nothing to do with the day of the week. Morning and brunch weddings reduce catering costs by 30 to 40 percent, a bigger saving than any weekday discount on food, and a couple that moves the ceremony to 11 a.m. on a Saturday keeps the guest list and cuts the catering bill. The day of the week is the most visible saving. It is not the only one, and for some couples it is not the best one.

The implication is a calculation, not a preference. Ask every venue on the shortlist for its Friday, Sunday, and Monday-to-Thursday pricing in writing, and compare the same room on four different days before deciding anything. Weigh the venue saving — $2,000 to $3,000 at the published rates — against the guests who will not come, and against the vendors who become available. If the saving buys the video package, upgrades the bar, or makes the venue affordable at all, take the weekday and spend the difference on the day itself. If the saving is $400 and the guest list loses a third of its names, keep the Saturday. The calendar is the last unpriced arbitrage in weddings. Like every arbitrage, it pays the people who measure it and punishes the people who assume it.
