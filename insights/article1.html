The Second "I Do": Inside the Vow Renewal Economy

The chapel holds forty people — about thirty-five more than the couple had at their first wedding. They have been married twenty-five years. They are doing it again on purpose.

Prime Domain Capital Insights · October 27, 2026 · 5 min read

The chapel holds forty people, which is about thirty-five more than the couple had at their first wedding. She wears a dress she chose in a week, not a year. He wears a suit he already owned. Their daughter reads something she wrote on her phone in the car on the way over, and when she cries, nobody pretends it is a surprise. They have been married twenty-five years. They are doing it again on purpose, in front of witnesses, in Las Vegas, on a Saturday afternoon, because the first time around they were twenty-two and broke and the photographs came back blurry.

This is the vow renewal economy, and it is the wedding industry's strangest growth story: a product nobody needs, bought by people who already own it, in a market where the main event costs more every year.

The numbers explain why chapels and resorts have started taking renewals seriously. A renewal package at Vegas Weddings, one of the city's long-running chapel operators, starts at $159 and runs to $1,449 for its most elaborate offering. Graceland Wedding Chapel sells Elvis-themed ceremonies starting at $183. These are not first-wedding prices. They are deliberately not first-wedding prices. The Knot's 2024 Real Weddings Study put the average American wedding at $33,000, and Zola's 2026 Cost Index put it at $36,000. The Wedding Report, which surveys actual couples rather than vendors, put the 2024 median at $13,195. The gap between the average and the median tells its own story: a small number of very expensive weddings drags the average up, while most couples spend far less than the headlines suggest. Renewals sit below even that median, and that is the point. A couple renewing at twenty-five years is not financing a beginning. They are buying an afternoon.

The venue side of the trade has numbers to match. Peerspace, the marketplace for renting event spaces by the hour, lists more than sixty Las Vegas venues positioned for vow renewals, averaging $155 an hour, with a typical booking of six hours for forty-five guests and an average rating of 4.87 stars. That is a real category on a real platform, not a filter tag somebody added as a joke. Sandals Resorts, which built its business on honeymoons, runs a standing Renew Your Vows offer across its properties and has spent $200 million transforming its resorts through the end of 2026. When a company that sells romance for a living spends that kind of money on its properties, it is not guessing about demand. It is following it.

The demand itself is not mysterious. About two million Americans marry every year, and the wedding market as a whole runs somewhere between $64.9 billion and $66.2 billion by the estimates cited in recent investment research. First weddings, though, are a young person's market, and young people are marrying later and spending under more pressure. Renewals invert the customer. The buyer is older, wealthier, and already convinced of the product. Nobody has to sell a forty-year-old couple on marriage. They have done it. The pitch is narrower and, for a vendor, cheaper: you do not have to find the couple, convince the parents, coordinate the registry, and survive the seating chart. You have to give two people a good reason to gather their favorite people and say the words again, better, with better photographs.

That inversion is what makes renewals interesting as a business rather than a curiosity. A first wedding is a once-in-a-lifetime purchase with a brutal acquisition cost: vendors compete for a customer who will never buy again, which is why wedding marketing is so frantic and so expensive. A renewal customer is the opposite. They return. They bring their children, who are themselves approaching marrying age. They post photographs that do the vendor's advertising for free, because a twenty-five-year anniversary photograph is the rare piece of marketing content people actually want to share. The economics of the second ceremony look nothing like the economics of the first, and vendors who treat renewals as a discounted first wedding are leaving the better business on the table.

The honest objection is that renewals are still small. They are a fraction of first weddings, and most chapels still treat them as a package variation — the same ceremony with "renewal" swapped into the brochure — rather than a business of their own. That is true, and it is also the opportunity. Markets with no incumbent brand are rare in an industry as old and as marketed as weddings. The renewal trade has chapels, resorts, and venues, but it does not have a name that stands for renewals the way a handful of names stand for first weddings. Couples searching for a renewal find a list of add-ons. They do not find a destination.

There is a second objection worth stating plainly: renewal demand has a ceiling that first-wedding demand does not. Every renewal requires a marriage that lasted, and marriages do not all last. The renewal market is, by definition, a subset of the married population that stayed married and stayed happy enough to celebrate it. That is a real constraint, and it means renewals will never match first weddings in volume. But volume is the wrong measure. The renewal buyer spends with less friction, returns more often, and costs less to acquire. A smaller market with better customers is not a consolation prize. In most businesses it is the whole strategy.

So the question for anyone building in this corner of the trade is not whether renewals are big enough. It is whether anyone will brand them as a category before the big platforms notice the numbers and do it themselves. The couple in the chapel on Saturday afternoon did not buy a discount wedding. They bought a second beginning, deliberately, with witnesses. The operator who understands that distinction — and builds a name around it instead of a package variation — will own a market that currently has no owner.
